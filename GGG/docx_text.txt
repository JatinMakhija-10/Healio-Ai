Executive SummaryArovia.AI has a genuinely unusual amount of real clinical-AI architecture already in place for its stage: a Bayesian/MCMC diagnostic engine with gender- and age-conditioned priors, a deterministic persona parser for BMI/frailty/polypharmacy, dual-source RAG retrieval across homeopathic and Ayurvedic knowledge bases, and a multi-layer safety-guard system with posterior red-flag escalation. That is not a small foundation, and it shows in how quickly the team correctly root-caused the demographic-gating bug that prompted this audit.The honest verdict is that Arovia is not yet a world-class AI doctor, and nothing reviewed here suggests it&apos;s close to safe for unsupervised deployment to millions of users. It is, however, a well-architected early-stage clinical AI product with a clear, buildable path to that bar — provided the next phase of work treats clinical safety testing, memory persistence, and regulatory classification as first-class engineering problems rather than follow-on cleanup. Section 17 (Final Verdict) makes this case in full; the short version is that the gap between where Arovia is and where it needs to be is real but not structural — it is a gap of validated coverage (tests, golden datasets, red-teaming), not of fundamentally wrong architecture.The five findings that matter mostThe gender-gating fix, as currently scoped, closes the embarrassing bug but not the dangerous one. Gating pregnancy-related screening on the gender field rather than reproductive anatomy fixes cisgender men being asked an odd question, but risks silently suppressing a life-threatening screen for transgender men. This is the single highest-priority clinical-safety item in this report (Section 5).There is no persistent, decay-aware memory across sessions yet. An allergy or major diagnosis mentioned once is not reliably retrievable later. This is a patient-safety gap before it&apos;s a personalization gap (Section 6).“FDA/SaMD readiness” is the wrong regulatory target for where this product actually operates. As of early 2026, India&apos;s CDSCO has made AI diagnostic-software licensing mandatory, and Arovia&apos;s diagnosis-presenting design is a real candidate for the higher-scrutiny Class C tier. This needs to be the compliance priority, not a US framework that may not apply for years, if ever (Section 13).No clinical-safety-specific test suite or golden dataset exists yet as a CI gate. General code-correctness testing and clinical-behavior testing are different disciplines, and only the former appears to be in place. This is the single highest-leverage process gap in the whole audit (Section 14).The architecture itself is sound; what&apos;s missing is validated coverage, not a redesign. Nearly every gap in this report is closeable by extending patterns Arovia already has (the safety-guard layers, the output validator, the persona engine) rather than by rebuilding them.This report follows the structure of the audit brief you provided, reorganized into eighteen sections for readability, plus a companion Excel workbook holding the three enumerable deliverables: a Top 100 Problems list, a Top 100 Improvements list, and a 100-case Testing Strategy sheet, each organized into ten categories of ten items so that scope and quality both hold up at that volume. Read Section 2 (Methodology) before anything else — it explains exactly what in this report is grounded in what you&apos;ve shared versus general clinical-AI-safety expertise applied to your architecture, which matters for how you weight every claim that follows.2. Architecture ReviewArovia&apos;s architecture is a multi-hop pipeline: an authenticated Next.js/TypeScript API route resolves the user&apos;s profile, runs dual-source RAG retrieval (Jina embeddings over a Boericke homeopathic knowledge base, Gemini embeddings over an Ayurvedic knowledge base) alongside a deterministic persona parser, feeds all of it into a Bayesian/MCMC diagnostic engine and a rule-based safety-guard layer, and finally generates a response via Groq (Llama-3.3-70B) with a Gemini 2.5 Flash fallback. This is a genuinely sophisticated shape for an early-stage product — most symptom-checker MVPs skip straight to “prompt an LLM with the symptoms” and never build the deterministic core at all.What&apos;s structurally rightA deterministic diagnostic core (MCMC engine, persona parser, safety guards) sitting underneath an LLM formatting layer is the correct architecture for a clinical product — it means diagnostic substance doesn&apos;t have to be re-earned from the LLM on every call, only its presentation does. Section 9 (Clinical Decision Engine) argues this split should be enforced more strictly than it currently appears to be, but the underlying instinct is right.Separating knowledge retrieval (RAG) from diagnostic reasoning (MCMC) from safety gating (guard layer) rather than asking one LLM call to do all three is a real architectural strength — it&apos;s what makes each layer independently testable, which Section 14 leans on heavily.A fallback LLM provider (Gemini 2.5 Flash behind Groq/Llama-3.3-70B) is good resilience engineering, though Problem P045 flags that its safety behavior needs independent validation, not assumed parity.Where the architecture has real gapsThe Top 100 Problems workbook (Category: Architecture & Security) enumerates these in full; the ones worth naming here because they cut across the whole system:Trust boundary on the client-supplied profile (P015). The diagnose route reportedly serializes whatever profile object the client sends rather than resolving it authoritatively server-side. This is the architectural root of a meaningful share of the demographic-gating bug&apos;s blast radius, and it&apos;s a pattern worth auditing everywhere else client data feeds a safety-relevant decision.No confirmed hop-level observability (P068). A four-stage pipeline (RAG → MCMC → LLM → validation) without distributed tracing means every production incident starts with “which of the four systems did this?” instead of an answer.Security posture is described, not evidenced (P062). Supabase RLS and token auth are the right building blocks, but RLS misconfiguration is one of the most common real-world sources of cross-tenant data leakage precisely because it&apos;s easy to get subtly wrong — this warrants an RLS-specific review, not just a general pentest.No load-testing evidence for the full multi-hop pipeline under concurrency (P067). Serverless scalability is a compute-elasticity property; it doesn&apos;t guarantee the pipeline&apos;s end-to-end latency holds up under real concurrent conversational load.Patient-experience and clinical-workflow architectureThe prior 23-issue UI/UX audit already covers interface-level patient experience in depth, so this report doesn&apos;t repeat it. What it adds is the content and communication layer sitting on top of the interface — how uncertainty, urgency, and escalation get worded — covered in Section 10 (Explainability) and as the “Trust & Communication” category in the Top 100 lists, since that&apos;s a distinct discipline from visual/component design and wasn&apos;t in scope for that earlier audit.4. Clinical Intelligence & Diagnostic ReasoningThe brief asks a direct question: does this reason like an experienced physician? The honest answer is that the architecture reasons like a well-instrumented resident with a good textbook and an inconsistent attending looking over their shoulder — the individual components (Bayesian updating, gender/age-conditioned priors, red-flag escalation) are the right primitives a senior clinician&apos;s reasoning actually rests on, but several of the judgment calls a consultant makes automatically don&apos;t yet appear to be enforced as explicit rules.Where it behaves like a strong residentDifferential generation via Bayesian/MCMC posterior updating is fundamentally the right mechanism — real diagnostic reasoning is probabilistic, not rule-lookup, and building on MCMC rather than a decision tree is a meaningfully better foundation than most consumer symptom checkers use.Gender- and age-conditioned priors (the genderFilter mechanism, PersonaEngine&apos;s BMI/frailty adjustments) show real understanding that demographics shift base rates, not just presentation — this is exactly the kind of prior-conditioning a good clinician does automatically.The safety-guard escalation tiers (L1 safe through L5 emergency) show explicit awareness that diagnostic confidence and clinical urgency are different axes that both need to be tracked — a system that only optimized for “most likely diagnosis” would be missing this entirely.Where it doesn&apos;t yet reach consultant-level judgmentThese map directly to the Diagnostic Reasoning category in the Top 100 Problems workbook:Cannot-miss diagnoses aren&apos;t evidenced as a protected category (P002, P027). A senior clinician actively rules out ACS, PE, aortic dissection, meningitis, sepsis, ectopic pregnancy, and testicular torsion for compatible presentations regardless of their prevalence-weighted probability, precisely because base-rate reasoning under-weights rare-but-lethal conditions. A pure MCMC posterior can mathematically round these to near-zero and still be statistically defensible while being clinically wrong.Atypical presentation handling isn&apos;t evidenced as a distinct layer (P028). Silent MI in diabetics and atypical stroke presentation in women are the textbook examples of where symptom-matching alone under-triages exactly the patients most at risk of being under-triaged elsewhere in the healthcare system too. This needs an explicit rule layer referencing AHA/ACC and equivalent stroke-recognition guidance, not just better base-rate math.Evidence retraction isn&apos;t confirmed (P021). A consultant updates their thinking when a patient corrects themselves; whether the MCMC engine recomputes from a corrected evidence set or just accumulates the correction on top of the original evidence isn&apos;t evidenced either way.Chronology and onset order aren&apos;t confirmed as first-class inputs (P026). Sequence is one of the most clinically informative things a patient can report — rash-then-fever and fever-then-rash suggest genuinely different differentials — and a system that treats symptoms as an unordered set is discarding real signal.Comorbidity / concurrent-condition reasoning isn&apos;t evidenced (P023). Real patients, especially with existing chronic conditions, often have more than one active issue; a differential optimized for “most likely single disease” isn&apos;t the same task as “most likely explanation for this patient&apos;s actual situation.”Prevalence priors&apos; geographic calibration is unconfirmed (P022). This is likely the single highest-leverage accuracy lever available: MCMC posteriors are extremely base-rate-sensitive, and India-specific epidemiology (TB, anemia, and several infectious-disease base rates in particular) diverges meaningfully from Western datasets a global or US-sourced prior table would assume.Confidence, uncertainty, and evidence weightingOne gap worth its own paragraph: nothing in the reviewed materials indicates the system distinguishes a 60% posterior built on two symptoms from a 60% posterior built on fifteen. Statistically these are very different levels of confidence in the estimate itself, even when the point estimate is identical, and presenting them identically to a patient invites over-trust in early-conversation, low-evidence outputs specifically — exactly the moment a symptom checker is least equipped to be confident. Surfacing an explicit “limited information so far” qualifier below a defined evidence-volume threshold (Problem P025) is a low-effort, high-value fix.How this compares across the medical-student-to-professor spectrumCapabilityWhere Arovia sitsWhat consultant-level would addGenerate a differentialStrong — MCMC-based, demographically conditionedComorbidity-aware joint reasoning, not just marginal per-disease probabilityPrioritize life-threatening conditionsPartial — escalation tiers existAn explicit, prevalence-independent “never-omit” layer for cannot-miss diagnosesUpdate on new evidencePartial — additive updating confirmed, retraction unconfirmedFull recomputation from a versioned evidence set each turnRecognize atypical presentationsNot evidenced as a distinct layerExplicit atypical-presentation rules for known high-risk patterns (diabetic MI, stroke in women)Handle uncertainty honestlyPoint estimates onlyEvidence-volume-aware confidence communication5. Patient Interview QualityThis is the section where the shared materials give the most direct, concrete evidence, because the bug that prompted this whole audit is a patient-interview-quality bug. The compound safety-question pattern in SymptomQuestionSchemas.ts — bundling “severe worsening, rigid belly, fainting, blood in stool or vomit, or pregnancy possibility” into one string — is worth examining as a specimen of a broader interview-design problem, not just the one bug it happened to cause.Would an experienced doctor ask these questions?A clinician taking a history asks red-flag questions as separable, individually-relevant items — never as a compound sentence where the patient has to mentally filter out the parts that don&apos;t apply to them. The compound-string pattern isn&apos;t just a gender-gating bug; it&apos;s evidence that the underlying question-authoring approach treats “all the red flags for this complaint” as one unit rather than as independently relevant clinical items. That&apos;s the deeper fix worth making, beyond the specific pregnancy clause: audit every compound question in the schema file for the same pattern, not just the one instance that got reported.What&apos;s missing from the interview flowNo stated rationale for sensitive questions (Problem P094). A pregnancy question with one attached clause of “we ask this because it changes what we should rule out” reads as far less intrusive than the same question asked cold — and gives the patient a natural cue if the question genuinely doesn&apos;t apply to them.No in-conversation “this doesn&apos;t apply to me” affordance (Problem P095). This is both a UX fix and a real-time bug-detection mechanism — it would have surfaced the original bug from user feedback long before it needed a formal audit to find it.No confirmed onset-order capture (Problem P026, Section 4). A good clinical interview asks not just what symptoms are present but in what order they appeared — this is interview design as much as it&apos;s a Bayesian-engine input issue.No confirmed handling of user pushback mid-interview (Problem P088, P098). A patient disagreeing with a question&apos;s relevance, or asserting their own self-diagnosis partway through, is a common real conversational branch that a good interviewer (human or AI) handles by acknowledging and continuing to reason independently — not by either arguing or simply complying.What&apos;s workingAge-stratified question logic (pediatric Reye&apos;s-syndrome exclusion, elderly anticholinergic/fall-risk screening) shows real adaptive design intent — the interview isn&apos;t static across all users, which is more than most symptom checkers manage.Longitudinal awareness (tracking unresolved symptoms across 3+ sessions) is a genuinely good interview-design idea most single-session symptom checkers don&apos;t attempt at all — Section 6 (Memory) covers what it needs to be reliable.7. Medical Safety ReviewThis is the section that matters most in the entire audit, and it&apos;s worth reading in full rather than skimming to the workbook. Everything else in this report — architecture, diagnostic reasoning, benchmarking — is in service of this question: what could this system do that actually hurts someone, and is that risk closed, mitigated, or open?7.1 The headline finding: anatomy-versus-identity gatingBoth technical write-ups you shared propose fixing the pregnancy-question bug by gating reproductive-health screening on the profile&apos;s gender field — suppressing pregnancy-related questions and conditions entirely whenever gender equals male. This is a real improvement over the current behavior, and the layered approach behind it (decompose the compound question, resolve gender authoritatively server-side, validate output post-generation) is sound engineering. It also encodes an assumption worth stating plainly: gender identity and reproductive anatomy are not the same variable, and a transgender man can retain a uterus and ovaries, menstruate, and become pregnant — including an ectopic pregnancy, which is a leading cause of first-trimester maternal mortality when missed.If the safety layer suppresses all pregnancy-related screening whenever gender equals male, the fix as scoped doesn&apos;t just fail to help this population — it actively removes a screen that generic, un-gated logic would have at least attempted. That is a more severe failure mode than the bug being fixed. The original bug produces an awkward, occasionally distressing question for cisgender men. The fix, as currently scoped, risks a missed emergency for a specific subset of male-identified patients. Neither this report nor a chat-based audit is the right final word on how to resolve this — it&apos;s a genuine clinical and product-ethics judgment call, and it belongs with your clinical advisors directly — but it needs to be an explicit decision, made with the tradeoff stated this plainly, not an implicit default of how the gender field happens to be checked.The fix that resolves this without reintroducing the original bug: separate a communication-gender field (which drives how the AI talks to someone, and can safely stay a simple field) from a reproductive-anatomy field (which drives what gets screened, and needs its own explicit, honestly-labeled value — including “unknown”). When anatomy is unknown, the safety layer should ask one direct, non-presumptive clarifying question rather than inferring it from gender identity in either direction. This is Problem P013 and Improvement I013 in the companion workbook, both marked Critical/P0 for exactly this reason.7.2 Systematic safety-domain reviewEscalation and emergency recognitionThe L1–L5 tiered escalation system with posterior-triggered guards (CP8/CP10) is real infrastructure most competing products at this stage don&apos;t have. What&apos;s unconfirmed is whether it&apos;s backed by validated triage scoring (NEWS2, pediatric NICE traffic-light bands, qSOFA-equivalent logic) or is closer to unstructured LLM judgment layered on top of the Bayesian posterior — Problem P001. This distinction matters enormously: validated scores exist specifically because free-text clinical judgment, human or AI, is measurably inconsistent across equivalent presentations, and a system aiming to be trustworthy at scale should be able to point to which validated instrument backs each escalation decision.Pregnancy, pediatric, and geriatric-specific risksPregnancy: beyond the anatomy-gating finding above, pregnancy status appears to be inferred once rather than tracked as persistent, re-confirmed session state (Problem P019) — a flag established early in a conversation needs to keep applying, not silently stop.Pediatric: the reviewed materials describe a single <12 age threshold for rules like Aspirin/Reye&apos;s exclusion, but NICE&apos;s own traffic-light system uses three distinct age bands (0–3 months, 3–36 months, >36 months) because the risk profile genuinely differs that much within “pediatric” — a febrile six-week-old and a febrile four-year-old are not the same triage decision (Problem P004).Geriatric: anticholinergic-burden and fall-risk logic is described as present via the frailty index, but its enforcement boundary — whether it&apos;s checked against every output surface including the RAG remedy layer, not just the LLM&apos;s own free text — isn&apos;t evidenced (Problem P016).Medication safety: dosage, interactions, contraindications, allergiesThis is the domain where hallucination risk and clinical risk overlap most dangerously. Nothing in the reviewed materials describes a guardrail preventing the LLM from generating a plausible-sounding but fabricated drug name or dosage when the retrieved context doesn&apos;t contain one — Problem P046, rated Critical. This is worth treating as a hard architectural constraint rather than a prompt instruction: any dosage or drug name in output should be pulled verbatim from a curated, versioned formulary lookup, never freely generated by the LLM, and this should be checked in the output-validation layer (Section 8), not just requested in the prompt. Separately, allergy cross-checking is described as present for the core diagnostic engine but not confirmed as covering the homeopathy/Ayurveda remedy layer specifically (Problem P007) — an allergy check that covers one output surface and not another is, from the patient&apos;s perspective, no different from not having one.False reassurance and over-reassuranceThe risk here is subtler than an outright missed diagnosis: a technically-correct “likely nothing serious” response without an explicit, specific safety-net instruction (“return if you develop X, Y, or Z”) is the pattern that fails when a genuinely rare disease doesn&apos;t behave as expected. The WHO/NICE convention of mandatory safety-netting language on every non-emergency response — not just high-severity ones — is a cheap, high-value fix (Problem P009) that&apos;s easy to deprioritize precisely because it only matters in the cases that don&apos;t look urgent yet.Crisis disclosures outside the product&apos;s designed scopeArovia is architected around physical-symptom triage, but any sufficiently-used chatbot will occasionally receive a disclosure of self-harm or suicidal ideation embedded in an otherwise physical-symptom message. Nothing in the reviewed materials describes a classifier that catches this before the diagnostic pipeline engages with it as a symptom-description problem to be differentiated. This needs to be a pre-pipeline check, not a downstream one — Problem P008, rated Critical.Drug-and-remedy claims under Indian advertising lawThis belongs in a safety review, not just the regulatory section, because the mechanism is the same one that causes the false-reassurance risk above: unsupported efficacy language. If the homeopathy/Ayurveda remedy layer&apos;s output can read as claiming to cure, treat, or diagnose a scheduled condition, that&apos;s both a clinical-safety concern (a remedy claim substituting for appropriate care-seeking) and a specific legal exposure under India&apos;s Drugs and Magic Remedies Act, detailed in Section 13. Memory & Personalization ReviewThe memory-engine design already scoped in the shared materials — vector retrieval with an importance-and-decay composite score, immutable categories exempted from decay, category-keyed conflict resolution that supersedes rather than merges contradicting facts — is a genuinely well-thought-out specification. The core formula (similarity × weighted blend of importance and decay × confidence) correctly captures the three things that should matter for what surfaces in a clinical memory system: is it relevant, is it important, and is it still current. This section takes that design as the right target architecture and focuses on what&apos;s needed to actually build and harden it.Why this is a safety issue, not just a personalization oneIt&apos;s worth being blunt about priority: the current gap — no persistent semantic memory across independent sessions — means a penicillin allergy or a major prior diagnosis disclosed once has no guaranteed way to resurface in an unrelated later session. Personalization gaps make the product worse. This kind of gap can make it actively unsafe, because the system can proceed with a differential or a remedy suggestion that a known allergy should have ruled out, simply because that allergy lived in a session that&apos;s no longer in context. This is Problem P031, rated Critical, and it should be treated with the same urgency as the demographic-gating fix that prompted this whole audit.What the design gets rightExempting an explicit “IMMUTABLE” category (allergies, chronic diagnoses, major surgeries, gender) from decay entirely, rather than tuning one global decay rate, is the correct approach — a single decay constant either keeps irrelevant old symptoms around too long or lets safety-critical facts fade, depending which way it&apos;s tuned (Problem P033).Category-keyed conflict resolution (a newer fact in the same category supersedes an older contradicting one, rather than both being retrieved and left for the LLM to reconcile) is exactly right — asking an LLM to silently arbitrate between “not on any medication” from March and “started metformin” from June, without explicit supersession logic, is a real source of avoidable inconsistency (Problem P032).What needs to be added before this shipsUser-facing access and correction (Problem P034). There&apos;s no described way for a user to see, correct, or delete what&apos;s been stored about them. Beyond the trust argument, this is very likely to become a data-principal-rights obligation under the DPDP Act (Section 13) — building it now is cheaper than retrofitting it under a compliance deadline.Provenance-aware weighting (Problem P036). The design doesn&apos;t yet distinguish memories the user explicitly stated from ones the system inferred from a pattern. An inferred “seems to get headaches on Mondays” shouldn&apos;t carry the same diagnostic weight as a stated “I&apos;m allergic to penicillin”, and conflating them risks a shaky inference influencing a future differential as if it were a direct disclosure.Multi-person / shared-account isolation (Problem P038). Family members sharing a device or account is a common real-world pattern this design doesn&apos;t yet address — without explicit per-profile partitioning, a child&apos;s session risks being diagnosed against a parent&apos;s stored allergy or medication history, or vice versa.Retention policy (Problem P040). No described lifecycle for memory categories that shouldn&apos;t persist indefinitely (a resolved acute complaint from two years ago). This is both a breach-exposure question and, under a data-minimization-conscious reading of DPDP, a compliance question.CRDT or timestamp-based reconciliation for the offline client cache (Problem P035). Without this, a stale local cache can silently overwrite a more recent server-side correction on multi-device use.9. Prompt Engineering ReviewThe seven-layer system-prompt hierarchy already sketched in the shared materials — identity, hard demographic constraints, deterministic diagnostic core, drug interactions, retrieved memory, conversation history, output instructions, in that order — is a strong instinct: put the non-negotiable safety constraints where they&apos;re least likely to be diluted by everything that comes after them. The gap is between designing that hierarchy and actually guaranteeing an LLM respects it, which position in the prompt alone doesn&apos;t do.Positional hierarchy is necessary, not sufficientModern LLMs don&apos;t reliably treat “earlier in the prompt” as “higher priority” without that priority being reinforced explicitly — and reinforced doesn&apos;t mean restated once, it means validated adversarially. Two specific risks follow directly from this (Problems P041 and P042): first, a sufficiently unusual phrasing or a determined multi-turn attempt could still get a lower-priority instruction to override a stated hard constraint unless that&apos;s been tested, not just designed; second, and more mundane, is that if retrieved memories plus RAG context plus conversation history ever exceed the usable context window, silent truncation needs to protect Layer 1/2 specifically — the safety-critical block should never be the thing that gets dropped because it wasn&apos;t pinned as always-included.RAG content is a distinct injection surfaceIt&apos;s easy to treat retrieved content differently from user input because it “feels” trusted — it came from your own knowledge base, not a stranger&apos;s message. But if Boericke and Ayurvedic retrieval results are concatenated directly into the prompt without being wrapped in explicit, clearly-untrusted delimiters, a crafted symptom description that gets matched against a manipulable or poisoned source is a real attack surface, distinct from and in addition to direct prompt injection via user messages (Problem P043). Both need adversarial testing — see the Prompt Injection category in the companion Testing Strategy sheet, which includes a case specifically targeting RAG-embedded instructions.Fallback-model parity is unverifiedGroq&apos;s Llama-3.3-70B and Gemini 2.5 Flash are different model families with potentially different instruction-following and safety-tuning characteristics. If the system prompt is shared verbatim between them — which the reviewed materials suggest — there&apos;s no guarantee that safety behavior validated against the primary model transfers cleanly to the fallback. This matters more than it might seem, because a fallback event is, by definition, already a degraded-state moment; it&apos;s exactly when you&apos;d most want confidence the safety layer still holds (Problem P045).Certainty language and hallucination-adjacent instructionsNo confirmed constraint against confident, non-hedged diagnostic language. “You have X” reads as authoritative to a lay user in a way that can short-circuit their judgment about whether to seek care, versus “X is one possibility, alongside Y and Z” (Problem P047).No confirmed scope-boundary instruction for out-of-scope requests. e.g., a user asking the system to interpret an uploaded lab report image, which is a materially different (and differently-regulated) task than symptom-based triage (Problem P048).Language-switching (English/Hinglish) shares the same generation call as clinical content. with no confirmed check that translation or code-switching can&apos;t quietly lose clinical specificity (Problem P049).VersioningNeither the system prompt nor the clinical knowledge base is described as versioned with a changelog. This isn&apos;t a nice-to-have — it&apos;s what makes it possible to answer “what exactly was this system instructed to do when it generated this specific past response,” which matters for internal incident review and will matter for CDSCO&apos;s Algorithm Change Protocol expectations once Arovia&apos;s SaMD classification is formalized (Section 13). This is a low-effort fix (Improvements I029, I050) that pays for itself the first time it&apos;s needed.10. Output Validation LayerThe validateOutputAgainstProfile() pattern already sketched in the shared materials — scanning generated output against the user&apos;s profile for demographic contradictions before it reaches the patient — is the right architectural instinct: catch what got through generation rather than relying on generation to be perfect. It&apos;s currently scoped narrowly (gender/pregnancy terms, pediatric aspirin), which is appropriately targeted at the bug that prompted it, but the pattern is worth generalizing before the next instance of this bug category shows up in a different guise.From a bug patch to a frameworkThe generalization is straightforward: turn the current if/else logic into a rule table — rows of (condition, prohibited terms, profile predicate) — so that adding coverage for a new demographic mismatch (say, a diabetes-medication caution that shouldn&apos;t apply to a profile with no diabetes flag) is a data change, not a code change. This is Problem P051 / Improvement I051, and it&apos;s the highest-leverage single change to this layer.A validator needs a defined remediation path, not just detectionThe current design catches a violation and returns isValid: false with a list of violations — but what happens next isn&apos;t specified in what&apos;s been shared. Does the system regenerate with the violation flagged in-context? Fall back to a safe templated response? Or, in the worst case, does the invalid response still reach the user with a warning attached? This needs to be an explicit, tested decision (Problem P052): regenerate once with the specific violation named in the retry prompt, and if that also fails, fall back to a safe non-LLM templated response rather than ever surfacing the flagged content. ExplainabilityThe brief&apos;s explainability checklist — why this disease, why not others, supporting and missing symptoms, confidence, evidence, risk factors, urgency, recommended tests, recommended next questions — is close to a complete specification for a good diagnostic-reasoning output schema. What determines whether Arovia can actually produce this reliably is whether the underlying data exists to explain, which connects directly to two gaps already flagged: likelihood-ratio provenance (Problem P024) and evidence-volume-aware confidence (Problem P025).A recommended explainability schemaEach differential-diagnosis item in the output should carry:Why this disease: the specific supporting symptoms/evidence that raised its posterior, named explicitly rather than implied by the diagnosis label alone.Why not others: at minimum, the top 1–2 alternative differentials and the specific evidence that ranked this one higher — this is what actually builds trust in a probabilistic system, more than the top diagnosis alone.Confidence, qualified by evidence volume: not just a percentage, but whether that percentage rests on 2 data points or 15 (Problem P025).Missing information that would sharpen the estimate: this doubles as the system&apos;s own justification for any follow-up question it asks next — an interview question with a stated clinical rationale (Problem P094) is this same information surfaced conversationally.Risk factors and urgency: explicitly separated from probability — a low-probability, high-urgency item (the cannot-miss diagnoses in Section 4) needs to be visually and structurally distinct from a high-probability, low-urgency one, not just ranked below it.Recommended next steps: tests or specialist type, paired with the general-physician-first fallback given uneven specialist access across India (Problem P055).None of this requires new infrastructure beyond what&apos;s already planned — it requires the knowledge base&apos;s likelihood ratios to carry source and confidence metadata (Section 4) and the output schema to surface that metadata rather than collapsing it into a single number. This is also, not incidentally, exactly the kind of structured reasoning trail a CDSCO SaMD review or a future clinical-safety incident investigation will want to see (Section 13).13. Regulatory Reality Check — India First, Not FDA FirstThe original brief asked for an “FDA/SaMD Readiness Assessment.” That&apos;s the wrong regulator for the market this product actually operates in today. This section explains why, based on India&apos;s own Software-as-a-Medical-Device framework, which has moved from draft guidance to mandatory licensing during the period covered by this audit. None of this section is legal advice — it&apos;s a factual map of a fast-moving regulatory area, intended to tell you exactly what to bring to counsel and why it&apos;s urgent, not to substitute for counsel&apos;s judgment.15.1 CDSCO SaMD classification is now mandatory, and Arovia is a plausible Class CIndia&apos;s Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation issued Medical Device Software guidance clarifying AI/ML-based diagnostic software&apos;s classification under the Medical Device Rules, 2017, with a four-tier risk framework (Class A, lowest risk, through Class D, highest). Reporting through early 2026 describes licensing under this framework as now mandatory for AI diagnostic software, following a period of draft guidance and reinforcement through 2026. Classification follows intended use — not just the underlying technology — and a product that presents a differential diagnosis, as Arovia&apos;s architecture is built to do, is a genuine candidate for at least Class C: guidance in this area specifically frames Class C as covering software that plays a role in diagnosing or managing conditions of moderate-to-high severity. Class C licensing in India runs through the central licensing authority rather than a state authority, and carries materially more rigorous review requirements, generally including an ISO 13485-aligned quality management system.15.2 Labeling and product copy are themselves a classification leverThis is the detail most product teams miss: the same underlying technology can land in a different classification tier depending on how it&apos;s described in labeling, marketing copy, and in-product language. Guidance in this area explicitly notes that wording changes suggesting diagnostic or life-supporting functionality can shift a product from a lower to a higher class. This cuts both ways for Arovia — it means product copy is a genuine, legitimate lever (alongside real safety work, not instead of it), and it&apos;s the direct regulatory rationale behind Problem P072/Improvement I072&apos;s recommendation to adopt explicit “triage and information, not diagnosis” framing in-product, mirroring how Ada Health frames itself (Section 13). This should be reviewed with counsel specifically for how it interacts with any “diagnosis” framing already built into the product&apos;s own naming and output structure.15.3 The DPDP Act: a real but finite runway, and one counterintuitive gapIndia&apos;s Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is being brought into force in phases: the Data Protection Board was established in November 2025, Consent Manager provisions take effect from November 2026, and the Act&apos;s substantive operational provisions — the ones with real day-to-day compliance implications — are set to take full effect in May 2027. That&apos;s a genuine window, not a reason to wait: 2026 is the period in which building the compliance posture is realistic, before the deadline where retrofitting it becomes urgent. Penalties are not proportional to company size — the maximum tier (reported at up to ₹250 crore for security-safeguard failures) applies regardless of stage.One nuance worth knowing specifically because it cuts against the GDPR/HIPAA-trained instinct most engineering teams default to: the DPDP Act, as currently structured, does not define a distinct “sensitive personal data” category with enhanced protections the way GDPR and HIPAA both do for health data specifically. That doesn&apos;t mean health data gets treated carelessly under DPDP — general obligations (purpose limitation, security safeguards, breach notification, data-principal rights) still apply in full — but a compliance plan built by directly translating GDPR “special category” assumptions risks being calibrated to the wrong framework. This is squarely a question for counsel to confirm current on, given this is an actively evolving area (Improvement I074).15.4 The homeopathy vertical carries its own, separate regulatory questionThe Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 restricts advertising claims of diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention for a schedule of roughly 56 conditions, including several (diabetes, cancer, asthma among them) that a symptom checker will routinely encounter. An AI-generated remedy suggestion that reads as a treatment or cure claim for a scheduled condition sits in territory worth resolving proactively. Separately, India&apos;s existing telemedicine guidance for homeopathic practice — issued for registered practitioners under the Central Council of Homoeopathy framework — is written for human-to-patient consultations, not for an AI system generating remedy suggestions without a registered practitioner in the loop. That&apos;s a genuine gap in existing guidance rather than a settled answer either way, and it&apos;s worth an explicit, written position from counsel rather than an assumption in either direction (Section 7.2, Problems P075/P076).15.5 Where FDA and other non-Indian frameworks actually fitFDA SaMD classification becomes relevant only if and when Arovia operates in or targets the US market — it isn&apos;t a live obligation for an India-first product today, and treating it as the primary regulatory question (as the original brief&apos;s framing did) risks spending scarce compliance effort on the wrong jurisdiction first. If US expansion is on the roadmap, FDA&apos;s own SaMD risk framework (which categorizes by the significance of the information provided to a healthcare decision and the state of the healthcare situation) is conceptually similar to CDSCO&apos;s tiering, so groundwork done for CDSCO classification — particularly clear intended-use documentation and a validated clinical-safety testing regime — will transfer usefully rather than being wasted effort.15.6 What to actually do with this sectionCommission a formal CDSCO SaMD/SiMD classification determination now — this is the single highest-priority action in this entire report, ahead of any specific feature fix, because operating an unclassified diagnostic-presenting product is a live exposure today, not a future one.Run a DPDP gap assessment in parallel — consent flows, breach-notification readiness, and data-principal access/correction rights (which overlaps directly with the memory-editing feature recommended in Section 8).Get a specific counsel review of the remedy layer&apos;s output against the Drugs and Magic Remedies Act schedule, independent of the general output-validation work already planned.Get an explicit written position on AI-generated (non-practitioner) homeopathic guidance under current AYUSH telemedicine guidance, revisited periodically given how actively this area is developing.Treat FDA readiness as a distinct, later workstream tied to an actual US-expansion decision, not a parallel priority to the above.16. Final VerdictCould Arovia become one of the world&apos;s best AI medical assistants?Yes — with a specific, honest caveat about what “best” has to mean for a product in this category. The architecture, the team&apos;s demonstrated instincts (the fact that this audit&apos;s biggest finding is a refinement of a fix the team had already correctly diagnosed, not a fix they&apos;d missed entirely), and the genuine differentiation of the Homeopathy/Ayurveda-plus-Bayesian-core combination all point toward a real, defensible path to being one of the best products in its specific category: an India-first, culturally-fluent, clinically-grounded symptom checker. “Best AI medical assistant in the world” in the sense of outperforming board-certified physicians at differential diagnosis is a different, much higher bar that no consumer symptom checker — including the best-funded global competitors reviewed in Section 13 — has convincingly cleared yet, and treating that as the near-term target risks distorting priorities away from the bar that actually matters first: being safe, honest about uncertainty, and genuinely useful for triage.What&apos;s stopping it right nowNot the architecture. Three things, in order of how much they&apos;d hurt if left unaddressed: an unresolved clinical-safety gap in the very fix meant to close the bug that prompted this audit (Section 7.1); no validated, CI-blocking testing regime standing behind any of the safety infrastructure that does exist (Section 16); and an unaddressed, now-mandatory regulatory classification in the product&apos;s home market (Section 15).
